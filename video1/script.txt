# COLD OPEN
[globocnik,belzec,railway]
In January of 1943, a short coded radio message left the city of Lublin, in occupied Poland. It was sent by an SS officer named Hermann Höfle, and it contained almost nothing but numbers. Letters for the names of four places. And beside each letter, a figure.

Added together, the figures came to one million, two hundred and seventy four thousand, one hundred and sixty six.

That was the number of people who had arrived, in the year nineteen forty two alone, at four camps run by a single secret operation. Three of them were small camps hidden in the forests of eastern Poland. Camps with almost no barracks. Camps with no factories. Camps that most people in Germany had never heard of, and that the men who ran them would later try to erase from the earth.

British codebreakers intercepted that message. They decoded it. And then, for more than half a century, it sat unrecognised in an archive, until two historians noticed what it was.

The man who received the report, and whose operation it described, was not one of the famous names of the Third Reich. He was not Himmler. He was not Heydrich. He never stood in the dock at Nuremberg. Most people have never heard his name.

His name was Odilo Globocnik. His friends in the SS called him Globus. And for two years, he ran the most secret and most deadly project of the entire war.

This is his dark story.

# CHAPTER ONE. A CHILD OF A VANISHING EMPIRE
[trieste_old,trieste_harbor,klagenfurt]
Odilo Globocnik was born on the twenty first of April, nineteen oh four, in Trieste. Today Trieste is an Italian city on the edge of the Adriatic. But in nineteen oh four it was the great seaport of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, a crowded, multilingual place where Italian, German and Slovene were spoken on the same street.

The family name itself tells part of the story. Globočnik is a Slovene name. His family came from the mixed borderlands of the old Habsburg monarchy, and his father had served the empire as a military man before taking a position in its civil service. It was a respectable, ordinary family of a kind that existed in their thousands, people whose loyalty belonged not to a nation, but to the emperor in Vienna.

And then that world disappeared.

When Odilo was ten years old, the empire went to war. When he was fourteen, it lost. In nineteen eighteen the Habsburg monarchy collapsed, and the map of central Europe was redrawn almost overnight. Trieste became Italian. Families like the Globocniks, who had been part of the ruling order, suddenly found themselves foreigners in the city where they lived.

The family moved north, into the Austrian province of Carinthia, and settled in the town of Klagenfurt.

Carinthia in those years was a wounded place. Its southern border was disputed with the new Kingdom of Yugoslavia, and in nineteen twenty the question was settled by a plebiscite. Armed volunteers had fought over these valleys. For a teenage boy who had just lost his homeland, the lesson was simple, and it was poisonous: borders are decided by force, and nations are things you fight for, against neighbours who speak a different language.

The irony would follow him all his life. A man with a Slavic surname, born in a city that was now Italian, would become one of the most extreme German nationalists in Austria.

As a young man, Globocnik trained in construction and engineering, and worked in the building trade in Carinthia. He was big, loud and energetic. People who knew him described a man who was charming when he wanted to be, crude when he did not, and endlessly ambitious. He had a talent for organising, for getting things built, and for making the right friends.

In those years, he found the movement that would give his anger a shape. He joined the Austrian Nazis.

# CHAPTER TWO. THE ILLEGAL NAZI
[klagenfurt,anschluss,vienna1938]
To understand how Globocnik rose, you have to understand that in Austria, for most of the nineteen thirties, being a Nazi was a crime.

In nineteen thirty three, Austria's government, under Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss, banned the Nazi party. The party went underground. Its members carried out a campaign of propaganda, intimidation and bombings, aimed at destroying the Austrian state from inside and delivering the country to Hitler's Germany.

Globocnik threw himself into this underground world. He became an organiser, a courier, a man who moved money and messages between the Austrian Nazis and their sponsors across the border in Germany. He was arrested by the Austrian authorities more than once, and spent time in prison for his political activity.

In nineteen thirty four, Austrian Nazis attempted a coup. They murdered Chancellor Dollfuss in his own office. The coup failed, and the movement was driven even deeper underground. It was in that same year that Globocnik joined the SS, the elite guard of the Nazi party, led by Heinrich Himmler.

For the next four years, he did what he did best. He built networks. He was trusted with secrets, and he kept them. When the decisive moment came in March of nineteen thirty eight, Globocnik was one of the men working behind the scenes as the Austrian state collapsed under German pressure.

On the twelfth of March, German troops crossed the border. There was no resistance. Within days, Hitler stood before a cheering crowd in Vienna and announced that his homeland had entered the German Reich. The Anschluss was complete.

For the illegal Nazis of Austria, it was the moment of reward. And Globocnik was rewarded more than almost anyone.

In May of nineteen thirty eight, at the age of thirty four, he was appointed Gauleiter of Vienna. The party boss of one of the great capitals of Europe. A city of nearly two million people. The former construction worker from Klagenfurt now held enormous power over the city where, only a generation earlier, the Habsburg emperors had ruled.

# CHAPTER THREE. THE FALL
[vienna1938,himmler]
He did not keep it for long.

Vienna in nineteen thirty eight was a city of plunder. In the weeks after the Anschluss, Vienna's Jewish community, one of the largest in Europe, was humiliated, beaten and robbed. Businesses were seized. Apartments were taken. Property changed hands through threats and forced sales. And the party officials who controlled this process had endless opportunities to enrich themselves and their friends.

Globocnik's time as Gauleiter was chaotic. He was not an administrator by temperament, and his office became known for disorder and favouritism. In January of nineteen thirty nine, he was removed. The official reason given was illegal speculation in foreign currency.

For most men, that would have been the end. A disgraced party official, fired from one of the most visible jobs in the Reich for corruption.

But Globocnik had something more valuable than a clean record. He had a protector. Heinrich Himmler liked him.

Himmler saw in Globocnik exactly the kind of man he needed. Loyal to the point of fanaticism. Energetic. Unburdened by doubt. A man who would do things that others found difficult, and who would owe everything to Himmler personally.

So Himmler did not discard him. He parked him. Globocnik was sent into the armed SS, and served in a junior role during the invasion of Poland in September of nineteen thirty nine.

And then, in November of nineteen thirty nine, Himmler gave him a new job, far from Vienna. Far from the eyes of the party. In the newly conquered east.

Odilo Globocnik became the SS and Police Leader for the district of Lublin.

# CHAPTER FOUR. THE KINGDOM OF LUBLIN
[lublin,lublin_castle,frank,trawniki]
After Germany and the Soviet Union divided Poland between them in nineteen thirty nine, the central part of the country that was not annexed directly into Germany became a colony called the General Government. It was ruled from Kraków by a lawyer named Hans Frank.

Lublin was the easternmost district of that colony. A region of farms, forests, small towns and old cities, with one of the densest Jewish populations in Europe.

In the plans of the Nazi leadership, Lublin was not just a place. It was a laboratory.

In the first months of the occupation, German officials talked about turning the area into a kind of dumping ground, a so-called reservation into which Jews from across the Reich could be deported. Trains were sent. Thousands of people were pushed into the region with nothing. The scheme was chaotic, and it soon collapsed. But it revealed how the Nazi state already thought about this land: as an empty space, to be emptied further, and then refilled with Germans.

Globocnik embraced this vision completely. He dreamed of German settlement in the east, of SS strongpoints and colonies, of a frontier he would personally build.

He also built a power base. As SS and Police Leader, he answered not to Hans Frank, the civilian governor, but to Himmler. The two men, Frank and Globocnik, came to despise each other. Frank wanted to control everything in his General Government. Globocnik ignored him, went around him, and reported directly to the Reichsführer SS. In Frank's administration, complaints piled up about Globocnik's private empire, his rough methods and his contempt for rules.

None of it mattered. Himmler protected him.

From nineteen forty, Globocnik's office used Jewish forced labour on a massive scale, in labour camps across the district, digging ditches and building fortifications along the new border with the Soviet Union. Thousands of prisoners were worked, starved and beaten in these camps. It was a grim preview of what was coming.

In nineteen forty one, Globocnik created something else that would become essential to his work. At a former sugar factory in the village of Trawniki, his staff set up a training camp. The men trained there were mostly Soviet prisoners of war, recruited from camps where captured Red Army soldiers were dying of hunger by the hundreds of thousands. They were offered survival in exchange for service. They became guards. They became known as the Trawniki men.

In July of nineteen forty one, weeks after Germany invaded the Soviet Union, Himmler visited Lublin. He ordered the construction of a large SS camp on the edge of the city. It would become known as Majdanek.

By the autumn of nineteen forty one, Globocnik controlled an apparatus of camps, guards, labour and police across an entire district. He was trusted by Himmler, hated by the civil administration, and answerable to almost no one.

He was exactly the man for what came next.

# CHAPTER FIVE. THE SECRET ORDER
[himmler,globocnik,forest]
In the autumn of nineteen forty one, the war in the east was changing the plans of the Nazi leadership.

Behind the advancing German armies in the Soviet Union, mobile killing units were already shooting Jewish men, women and children by the tens of thousands. In the occupied territories of Poland, millions of Jews were crowded into ghettos, starving and dying of disease. Inside the Nazi leadership, the question was being discussed openly: what would be done with them?

In October of nineteen forty one, Himmler met with Globocnik. No full record of their conversation has survived. But what followed from that meeting is not in doubt. Within weeks, construction began on a new kind of camp in the Lublin district, near the small town of Bełżec.

It was not a labour camp. It was not a prison. It was a place designed for one purpose only.

To build it, Globocnik drew on a group of men with very specific experience. Since nineteen thirty nine, a secret program in Germany, known by the code name T4, had been murdering disabled people, patients in psychiatric hospitals and care homes, using gas. Tens of thousands of people had been killed. The staff of that program, chemists, guards, drivers and administrators, had learned how to kill large numbers of people quickly, and how to hide it.

In late nineteen forty one and early nineteen forty two, around a hundred of these men were transferred to Lublin, to work for Globocnik.

The most important of them was a former police officer named Christian Wirth. He had helped run the T4 killings. He was brutal even by the standards of the men around him, and he became the first commandant of Bełżec.

The operation that Globocnik now led would later be named after Reinhard Heydrich, the head of the Reich security apparatus, who was assassinated in Prague in the summer of nineteen forty two. Its name was Aktion Reinhard. Operation Reinhard.

Its goal was the murder of the Jews of the General Government. Around two million human beings.

# CHAPTER SIX. OPERATION REINHARD
[belzec,lublin_ghetto,deport,railway]
In the middle of March nineteen forty two, the deportations from the Lublin ghetto began. Trains left the city for Bełżec.

The Reinhard camps were unlike anything that had existed before. They were small. Bełżec covered an area you could walk across in a few minutes. There were very few prisoners kept alive, only small groups of Jewish workers forced to do the labour of the camp, who were themselves regularly killed and replaced.

People arriving on the trains were told they had reached a transit camp. They were told they would be washed and disinfected before being sent on to work. Within hours of arrival, almost all of them were dead.

The deception was deliberate. It was part of the design. Fear caused resistance, and resistance caused delay. So the camps were built to look like stations, and the killing was disguised as hygiene.

In May of nineteen forty two, a second camp began operating, at Sobibór, near the river Bug. In July, a third, at Treblinka, northeast of Warsaw. On the twenty second of July nineteen forty two, the great deportation from the Warsaw ghetto began. Within about two months, more than a quarter of a million people from Warsaw were taken to Treblinka.

Across the General Government, ghetto after ghetto was emptied. Small towns. Large cities. The trains ran to three destinations in the forests, and came back empty.

Globocnik's headquarters in Lublin managed it all. His staff organised the deportations together with the local police and civil authorities, scheduled the transports, supplied the guards, and supervised the camps through Wirth, who was eventually promoted to inspector of all three.

This was the part of the Holocaust that was most deliberately hidden. Auschwitz, with its survivors and its records, would become the symbol of the genocide. But Operation Reinhard left almost no survivors. Of the hundreds of thousands taken to Bełżec, only a handful are known to have survived the war.

Historians today estimate that Operation Reinhard took the lives of roughly one and a half to two million people. The most commonly cited figure is around one point seven million. Nearly all of them were Jews from Poland, along with Jews deported from across Europe, and smaller numbers of Roma.

That is more people than were murdered at Auschwitz.

# CHAPTER SEVEN. THE WITNESSES
[belzec,globocnik,forest]
Almost everything about Operation Reinhard was meant to stay invisible. And yet a few witnesses did see inside it, and lived to describe what they saw.

One of them was an SS officer. His name was Kurt Gerstein. He was a trained engineer and a specialist in disinfection, and he had joined the SS with deeply mixed motives. He was a devout Protestant, and he later claimed he had entered the organisation partly to find out what was really happening to people who disappeared.

In August of nineteen forty two, Gerstein was sent east with a delivery of prussic acid, the poison used for disinfection. In Lublin he was received by Globocnik himself. According to Gerstein's later account, Globocnik spoke openly and proudly about the operation, described it as one of the most secret matters in the Reich, and warned that anyone who talked about it would be shot.

Gerstein then travelled to Bełżec. There, with Christian Wirth beside him, he watched the arrival of a transport, and he witnessed the murder of the people on it. He described the scene in a long, detailed report that he wrote in the spring of nineteen forty five, after he surrendered to the French.

In that report, Gerstein recorded a remark he attributed to Globocnik. When someone suggested that it might be better to burn the bodies rather than bury them, because a later generation might not understand, Globocnik is said to have replied that, on the contrary, they should bury bronze tablets recording that it was they who had had the courage to carry out this great task.

We cannot be certain of every word in Gerstein's account. He wrote it from memory, in French custody, and he died in custody in July nineteen forty five, before he could be questioned in court. But the core of what he described has been confirmed again and again, by other testimony, by documents, and by the excavations at the camp sites.

The other witness was a prisoner. His name was Rudolf Reder. He was a soap manufacturer from Lwów, in his sixties, deported to Bełżec in August of nineteen forty two. He was pulled out of the transport to work, as one of the small group of prisoners forced to keep the camp running.

For about four months, Reder lived inside the machinery of death. He dug graves. He saw transport after transport arrive and disappear. In November of nineteen forty two, while being taken under guard to the town of Lwów to collect supplies, he managed to slip away from his guard and hid until the end of the war.

In nineteen forty six, Rudolf Reder published a short book about Bełżec. It is one of the very few eyewitness accounts from a prisoner of that camp. For hundreds of thousands of people murdered there, his testimony is almost the only voice that came back.

# CHAPTER EIGHT. CHAOS AT TREBLINKA
[treblinka,railway,deport]
Not everything in Globocnik's operation ran with the cold efficiency its planners wanted.

The first commandant of Treblinka was a doctor named Irmfried Eberl, another veteran of the T4 program. When the deportations from Warsaw began in late July of nineteen forty two, Treblinka was overwhelmed. Transports arrived faster than the camp could process them. Bodies lay along the railway platform. The smell could be noticed for kilometres around. Trains waited on the line for hours and days, with people dying inside the cars.

For Globocnik and Wirth, this was unacceptable. Not because of the suffering, but because it threatened the secrecy of the operation, and because it was disorder. In late August of nineteen forty two, Eberl was removed from his post. Deportations to Treblinka were briefly halted. Wirth arrived to reorganise the camp.

The man chosen to take over was an Austrian police officer named Franz Stangl, who had previously been commandant at Sobibór. Under Stangl, Treblinka was rebuilt to be larger and more efficient. A fake railway station was constructed, with a painted clock and signs for connecting trains, so that arriving deportees would believe they had reached an ordinary transit stop.

Decades later, in a West German prison, Stangl gave a long series of interviews to the journalist Gitta Sereny. Her book about him, Into That Darkness, is one of the most important studies ever written about how ordinary officials became mass murderers. In those interviews, Stangl described how he was first brought into the operation in Lublin, and how Globocnik and Wirth were the men who set the tone and gave the orders.

Treblinka became the deadliest of the three Reinhard camps. Historians estimate that between seven hundred thousand and nine hundred thousand people were murdered there in a little over a year.

# CHAPTER NINE. THE TELEGRAM
[telegram,hofle,belzec]
And that brings us back to the message from the beginning of this story.

Hermann Höfle was Globocnik's chief of staff for the deportations. Like his boss, he was an Austrian, from Salzburg. In January of nineteen forty three, he sent a radio report to Berlin, summarising the arrivals at the Reinhard camps up to the end of nineteen forty two.

The message used a single letter for each camp. L for Lublin, meaning Majdanek. B for Bełżec. S for Sobibór. T for Treblinka. Beside each letter was a number. The total was one million, two hundred and seventy four thousand, one hundred and sixty six.

What the senders did not know was that British intelligence was listening. German police radio traffic was being intercepted and decoded. The Höfle message was among them.

But the analysts who processed it had no way of understanding what those letters and numbers meant. It was filed away with countless other decrypts. It stayed in the files for decades.

Only in the year two thousand, after the documents were released, did historians recognise it for what it was: a precise bureaucratic accounting, from the inside, of the scale of the killing in a single year. A total written by the perpetrators themselves.

It is one of the most chilling documents of the twentieth century. And it was addressed to the operation that Odilo Globocnik ran.

# CHAPTER TEN. THE BUSINESS OF MURDER
[reichsbank,lublin,globocnik]
There was another side to Operation Reinhard, one that Globocnik took extremely seriously. Money.

Everything the victims brought with them was taken. Suitcases. Clothing. Shoes. Watches. Wedding rings. Currency. Gold teeth. All of it was collected, sorted and stored in warehouses in Lublin and at the camps.

Valuables were sent to Berlin, where they were deposited at the Reichsbank under a false name. Clothing was distributed to German settlers and to welfare organisations inside the Reich. Hundreds of freight cars of goods were shipped west.

At the end of the operation, Globocnik submitted a report on the economic results. By his own accounting, the value of the property taken added up to around one hundred and eighty million Reichsmarks. Historians believe the true figure was higher, because so much was stolen along the way by guards, officials and SS men, Globocnik's own circle included.

The man who had been fired as Gauleiter of Vienna for currency speculation now presided over one of the largest robberies in human history. And he wanted credit for it. In his reports and letters, he presented himself as a capable manager who had delivered results to the Reich. He asked for recognition for his men. He asked for medals.

# CHAPTER ELEVEN. EMPTYING THE LAND
[zamosc,lublin,forest]
Globocnik's ambitions did not stop with the murder of the Jews. He wanted to remake the land itself.

In November of nineteen forty two, while the Reinhard camps were still operating, he began another project in the southern part of the Lublin district, around the town of Zamość. The plan was to turn this region into a German settlement area, the first step in a vast program of colonisation in the east.

To do that, the Polish population had to be removed.

Over the following months, German forces expelled around one hundred thousand Polish villagers from their homes. Families were given minutes to leave. People were sorted by racial categories. Some were sent to forced labour in Germany. Some were sent to camps, including Auschwitz and Majdanek. Thousands of children were separated from their parents. Some of those children were judged racially suitable and taken for Germanisation. Many others died of cold, hunger and disease.

The Zamość expulsions provoked fierce resistance. Polish partisans attacked the German settlements, and the whole region slid into violence. Even other German officials, including Hans Frank, saw the operation as a disaster, because it drove Polish peasants into the arms of the resistance at a time when Germany desperately needed food and calm behind the front.

Globocnik pushed on anyway. It was what he believed in.

# CHAPTER TWELVE. REVOLT AND ERASURE
[treblinka,sobibor,sobibor_revolt]
By the spring of nineteen forty three, the work of Operation Reinhard was largely finished. Most of the Jews of the General Government were dead.

The Nazis now turned to hiding what they had done. At the camps, the mass graves were opened, and the bodies of the victims were burned on huge pyres, so that no trace would remain. Bełżec was closed and dismantled. Trees were planted over the site. A farmhouse was built there, and a guard was settled in it, to make the place look like ordinary countryside.

But at the remaining camps, the last prisoners understood exactly what was coming. They knew that when the work was done, they would be killed too. And they decided to fight.

On the second of August nineteen forty three, prisoners at Treblinka rose up. They seized weapons, set fire to parts of the camp, and a few hundred broke out. Most were hunted down and killed. But some survived, and later testified.

On the fourteenth of October nineteen forty three, prisoners at Sobibór, led by a Soviet Jewish officer named Alexander Pechersky and a Polish Jew named Leon Feldhendler, killed a number of SS men one by one in a carefully planned uprising, and around three hundred escaped into the forests. Several dozen of them lived to see the end of the war.

These revolts are among the most remarkable acts of resistance of the entire war. And they are a large part of the reason we know what happened inside these camps at all.

By then, Globocnik himself was already gone. In September of nineteen forty three, he was transferred away from Lublin. Weeks later, in early November, the SS carried out a massacre called Operation Harvest Festival, in which around forty thousand of the Jewish forced labourers still alive in the Lublin district were shot in a matter of days. It was the final act of the system he had built.

# CHAPTER THIRTEEN. TRIESTE
[trieste_harbor,sansabba,partisans]
Globocnik's new post took him back to where his life began.

In September of nineteen forty three, Italy surrendered to the Allies, and German forces occupied the north of the country. The region around Trieste, the city where Globocnik had been born, was placed under direct German control, as the Operational Zone of the Adriatic Coastland. Globocnik was appointed its Higher SS and Police Leader.

He did not come alone. He brought many of his Operation Reinhard men with him, including Christian Wirth.

In Trieste they turned an old rice-husking factory, the Risiera di San Sabba, into a police camp. It became a prison for partisans, political prisoners and Jews. Thousands passed through it on their way to Auschwitz and other camps. Thousands more were killed within its walls. Historians estimate that between three and five thousand people died there. It had a crematorium. It is the only camp on Italian soil known to have had one.

The region around Trieste was the scene of a brutal guerrilla war. Italian and Yugoslav partisans fought the German occupation in the mountains of the Karst and Istria. Globocnik's forces answered with the same methods they had used in Poland: reprisals, burned villages, executions.

It was here that Christian Wirth met his end. In May of nineteen forty four, he was killed in an ambush by Yugoslav partisans on a road near Trieste. The man who had commanded Bełżec died in a firefight on a country road, never facing a court.

# CHAPTER FOURTEEN. THE LAST HIDING PLACE
[alps,weissensee,paternion]
By the spring of nineteen forty five, the Reich was collapsing. As Allied and Yugoslav forces closed in on Trieste, Globocnik fled north, back into Carinthia, the province where he had grown up.

With him came a small group of his closest associates, including Hermann Höfle, the man who had sent the telegram, and other officers of his old staff. They hid in the mountains near the Weissensee, a long alpine lake, in a remote hut, hoping to disappear into the chaos of the surrender.

They did not disappear for long.

At the end of May nineteen forty five, a British army patrol tracked the group down and arrested them. The men were taken to the nearby town of Paternion for interrogation.

On the thirty first of May, nineteen forty five, shortly after his capture, Odilo Globocnik bit into a cyanide capsule. He died within minutes.

He was forty one years old.

He was buried in Paternion, outside the consecrated ground of the churchyard. In the years that followed, rumours circulated that he had escaped, that the dead man was someone else, that he had been spirited away by British intelligence. No serious evidence has ever supported those claims. The British soldiers who were there identified him, and the historical record is clear.

He never stood trial. He never answered a single question in court. He never had to face a single survivor.

# CHAPTER FIFTEEN. JUSTICE, LATE AND PARTIAL
[nuremberg,sobibor,treblinka]
Because Globocnik was dead, the task of answering for Operation Reinhard fell to the men who had served under him. Many of them simply went home after the war, returned to ordinary jobs, and lived quietly for years.

It was not until the nineteen sixties that West German prosecutors brought the Reinhard camps to court. The Bełżec trial in Munich ended with only one defendant convicted, and he received a sentence of four and a half years. The Treblinka trials in Düsseldorf brought heavier sentences, including life imprisonment for several of the guards. At the Sobibór trial in Hagen, some defendants were convicted, and others were acquitted.

Franz Stangl escaped Europe after the war, through Syria, to Brazil, where he worked under his own name at a car factory. He was tracked down and extradited to West Germany in nineteen sixty seven. In nineteen seventy he was sentenced to life imprisonment for his role at Treblinka. He died in prison the following year, only hours after his last conversation with Gitta Sereny.

Many survivors who testified at these trials were the people who had escaped during the uprisings. Without them, the courts would have had almost no witnesses at all.

# CHAPTER SIXTEEN. THE FORGOTTEN EXECUTIONER
[nuremberg,belzec,treblinka,sobibor]
After the war, the world learned the names of Hitler's inner circle. Göring. Himmler. Hess. Eichmann. Their faces became the faces of Nazi evil.

But Globocnik's name faded. He was dead before Nuremberg began. His operation had left few survivors, few documents, and few photographs. The camps had been ploughed over and planted with trees.

Many of his men were never punished at all. Some were tried decades later, in West German courts, in the nineteen sixties. Some received long sentences. Many received light ones. Some were never charged. Hermann Höfle was arrested in Austria in the nineteen sixties, and died in a Vienna prison cell before his trial could be completed.

For a long time, Operation Reinhard was one of the least known chapters of the Holocaust. Only in recent decades have the sites of Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka been properly excavated and turned into memorials. Archaeologists have found the remains of gas chambers and mass graves that the perpetrators believed they had erased forever.

The story of Odilo Globocnik is not the story of a genius, or a demon from another world. It is the story of an ordinary, ambitious, corrupt man, who lost a homeland as a child, found a cause that gave him purpose, and was handed almost unlimited power by people who knew exactly what he would do with it.

He was a builder. And what he built, in the forests of eastern Poland, was one of the greatest crimes in the history of humankind.

His name deserves to be remembered. Not for his sake. For theirs.
